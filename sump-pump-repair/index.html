Why a pump stops when you need it most

Pumps sit idle for months and then work hard in a storm. Most failures fall into a handful of causes.

Stuck float.

The float rises with the water and switches the pump on. If it snags on the pit wall or its own cord, the pump never starts.

Failed check valve.

This valve stops pumped water from falling back into the pit. A bad one makes the pump cycle over and over.

Clogged intake.

Silt, gravel, and debris can block the pump inlet, so the motor hums but moves little water.

Burned-out motor.

A pump that runs constantly or ran dry for long stretches wears out early.

Frozen or blocked discharge.

In winter the outlet pipe outside can ice over, which pushes the water right back into the pit.

Power loss.

Storms that knock out power also stop the pump. Without a battery backup, the pit fills quietly.

The pit is rising: what to do right now

Check the power first.

Look for a tripped breaker or an unplugged cord. Do this only if the floor around the outlet is dry.

Keep your hands out of the pit.

Never reach into water with a pump plugged in. Unplug it from a dry spot first.

Lift what you can.

Move boxes, furniture legs, and anything stored on the floor up onto shelves or blocks.

Bucket or wet vac as a bridge.

Bailing the pit buys time. Use only equipment that is rated for wet use, and keep cords up and dry.

Call and describe the sound.

Humming, clicking, silence, and gurgling each point at different parts, and it helps us bring the right ones.

Quick checks before the plumber arrives

These checks take little time and sometimes solve it. Stop and call if anything is wet near an outlet.

  1. Pour water into the pit.A bucket of water raises the float. A working pump starts. Silence points at power, float, or motor.
  2. Look at the float.Make sure it moves freely and is not wedged against the pit wall or caught on the discharge pipe.
  3. Listen to the motor.A hum with no water movement often means a clogged intake or a seized motor.
  4. Walk the discharge line outside.Look for a crushed, frozen, or disconnected pipe where the water should come out.

Where the water goes after it leaves the pit

A sump pump is only as good as its discharge. If the pipe empties right beside the foundation, the water seeps back down and the pump runs again, endlessly. Routing it well away from the wall, and onto ground that slopes away, keeps the pit from refilling with the same water.

The Colorado Geological Survey says poor surface drainage and irrigation water reaching soil beside foundations drive many soil problems across the state. That is a statewide finding, not a statement about your lot, but it has a practical meaning here: a pit that fills every time the sprinklers run is telling you where the water comes from.

Rules about where discharge may go can vary, so ask the Firestone building division before routing it toward the street or a neighbor. In winter, a short underground or buried section helps keep the outlet from icing. Front Range freezes make that worth planning in advance.

Sump pump or sewage ejector pump

They look similar and do different jobs, so the right repair depends on which one you have. A rural parcel around Mead may be on septic instead of town sewer, so tell us which you have before we plan an ejector repair. A sewage ejector is plumbing that the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC) as adopted in Colorado covers, so a licensed plumber should do the work.

Sump pump

  • Handles groundwater and clear drain water.
  • Sits in a pit that collects seepage.
  • Discharges outdoors, away from the house.
  • Usually has a float and a check valve.

Sewage ejector pump

  • Lifts wastewater from a basement bathroom or laundry.
  • Sits in a sealed pit with a vent.
  • Pushes waste up to the sewer or septic line.
  • Needs gas-tight lid and careful handling.

Battery backup: what to confirm

Storms often cut the power that the main pump depends on. A backup pump with a charged battery is the usual protection.

  • Battery age.Backup batteries lose strength over the years and should be tested on a schedule.
  • A separate float.The backup pump needs its own switch, mounted a little higher than the main one.
  • Its own discharge path.A backup that shares the main discharge needs a check valve of its own.
  • The alarm.A high-water or power-fail alarm gives a warning you can act on.
