A burst, a crack, and a drip are different problems

A true burst shows itself. Water sprays, the ceiling sags, or the floor is soaked within minutes. A crack or pinhole is quieter, and it can drip for weeks before anyone sees a stain. Both need repair, but only the first needs the main closed this minute.

Cracked and broken pipe repair starts by exposing the damaged section, cutting out the bad piece, and joining new pipe with fittings that suit the material. For a long split, the plumber often replaces a full length instead of patching it. A short section is faster to fix than people expect, and the wall or ceiling opening is kept as small as the pipe allows.

Pipe leak repair for a small seep follows the same path. The only difference is how much damage the water has already done, and how much of the wall has to come out to see it. Fittings and materials follow the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC) as adopted in Colorado, so ask the Firestone building division whether the repair needs a permit before the wall closes.

Where pipes split in a house

Burst pipes follow weak points. Knowing the usual ones helps you find the problem and describe it on the phone.

Exterior-wall pipes.

Lines in outside walls get the least heat, so they are first to freeze and split on a bitter night.

Garage and crawlspace runs.

Unheated spaces hold pipes that see cold air on every side. Splits show up as a wet slab or a dripping joist.

Hose bibs.

A hose left on the outdoor spigot can freeze the faucet and the pipe behind it, then leak inside the wall.

Old galvanized sections.

Original-townsite homes can have older steel supply pipe, which corrodes from the inside and lets go at a thread or joint.

Fixture supply lines.

Braided connectors under sinks and toilets wear out. A split there floods a cabinet floor quickly.

Drain pipes.

A cracked drain shows up as a wet floor only when water is sent down it, so it can hide for a long time.

How a frozen pipe turns into a burst

Firestone sits about 30 miles north of Denver at 4,856 feet, and Front Range winters swing between mild afternoons and hard overnight freezes. Water expands as it turns to ice, and the pressure builds in the pipe between the ice plug and the nearest closed faucet. That is where the split opens, and you may not see it until the ice melts.

If a tap runs slowly or not at all in cold weather, a frozen section is likely. Open that tap and the others on the line, then warm the pipe slowly with a hair dryer or warm towels. Never use a torch or open flame, because it can ignite framing or crack the pipe.

Do not thaw a pipe you can see is already split. Close the main and call. A thawed split pipe sprays the moment ice lets go, and the plumber can cut the damaged section out before that happens.

Frost depth is not a number we quote, and the exposure of each pipe matters more than any chart. Lines in exterior walls, unheated garages, and crawlspaces deserve the most attention before the first hard freeze.

Before the next hard freeze

  • Disconnect hoses.Remove garden hoses and drain outdoor spigots so water does not sit in the faucet.
  • Wrap exposed pipe.Foam sleeves on lines in garages, crawlspaces, and exterior walls slow heat loss.
  • Open cabinet doors.On a freezing night, let room air reach pipes under sinks on outside walls.
  • Let one tap drip.A thin steady drip on the line farthest from the main keeps water moving.
  • Know your shutoff.Find the main valve and try turning it now. A valve that will not close is worth a call before winter.
  • Heat empty rooms.Leave the thermostat set to a steady temperature if you travel. A cold house is where pipes split.

Fix the split or replace the run

The right call depends on the pipe and what else is wrong with it. When the whole house shows a pattern of failing lines, ask about replacing the failing runs before you pay for the same repair twice.

Spot repair makes sense when

  • The damage is one short section.
  • The rest of the pipe is clean and sound.
  • The split came from a single hard freeze.
  • The pipe is easy to reach.

Replacing the run makes sense when

  • Pinholes keep showing up in the same line.
  • The pipe is galvanized and corroded inside.
  • Several joints are leaking.
  • Walls are already open and more length is exposed.
