How to Vent a Tankless Water Heater in Firestone, CO

By the Firestone Pro Plumbing team. Published October 16, 2026. Updated October 16, 2026.

Talk to a plumber
(303) 552-3896
Call Now

A gas tankless water heater vents exhaust outdoors through a sealed or open pipe, depending on the model. Direct vent units pull air in from outside and push exhaust out. Atmospheric units use room air.

Pipe type, length, and clearances come from the maker's instructions and local code, not from guesswork. Call (303) 552-3896 and a licensed plumber can walk through the route with you.

Two venting styles and what they ask of your house

The unit's label tells you which style it is. The choice changes everything about the pipe.

Direct vent (sealed)

  • Draws combustion air from outdoors.
  • Pushes exhaust out through its own pipe.
  • Often uses a two-pipe or concentric kit.
  • Less affected by room air and tight homes.
  • Needs a wall or roof termination.

Atmospheric (room air)

  • Draws combustion air from the room.
  • Needs enough open air around the unit.
  • Exhaust goes up a flue.
  • Can pull on a tightly sealed house.
  • Less common on newer high-efficiency models.

Why venting is the part you cannot improvise

Exhaust from a gas burner carries carbon monoxide. If it leaks into the house or gets sucked back in, people get sick. That is why every maker publishes exact rules for pipe type, size, and length.

A vent that looks fine to the eye can still be wrong. The wrong pipe can warp from heat. Poor joints can leak exhaust. A termination in the wrong place can feed fumes back inside. Treat the manual as the first source and local code as the second.

Vent materials in general terms

The right choice is whatever the manual lists for your exact model.

How a vent route gets planned

A plumber follows a sequence like this before cutting a single hole.

  1. Read the manual.The model's instructions list pipe type, maximum length, number of elbows, and termination rules.
  2. Choose the exit.Pick a wall or roof exit that stays clear of windows, doors, vents, and walkways.
  3. Measure the run.Count straight pipe and every turn, because each one reduces the allowed length.
  4. Seal and support.Joints are made as the maker directs and the pipe is slope-checked and supported.
  5. Test the burner.After startup, the exhaust is checked and the unit is watched through a full cycle.

Clearances belong to the manual and the code

Distances from windows, doors, meters, and the ground are set by the manufacturer instructions and local code. We do not quote numbers here, because the wrong number from a web page can put exhaust where it should not be. Ask the plumber to show you the page in the manual that they used.

Snow and drifting matter on the Front Range. A termination too low to the ground can be covered after a storm, which blocks the exhaust. Ask where yours will sit and how it will be kept clear.

Condensate from high-efficiency units

High-efficiency models pull so much heat out of the exhaust that water vapor turns to liquid inside the unit. That liquid is acidic. It has to drain to a safe place, and a plain copper pipe or the wrong drain can be damaged by it.

Many installs use a small neutralizer cartridge that raises the pH before the water goes down a drain. In some spots, a pump is needed to lift it to a drain. Ask where yours will go and how the cartridge is checked.

Mistakes that show up on poorly vented installs

The most common error is a run that is too long or has too many turns. Each elbow works like extra pipe, so a route that looks short can break the maker's limit. The unit may still run, but it can shut down on error codes or burn poorly.

Another is pipe that slopes the wrong way. Condensate has to drain back toward the unit or a drain point, not pool in a low spot. A third is a termination placed near a window or an air intake, which lets exhaust drift back indoors.

A fourth is mixing parts from different makers. Vent parts are tested as a system with the heater, and an off-brand elbow can leak at the joint. If an installer suggests it, ask to see the manual page that allows it.

Warning signs that a vent needs attention

If you see any of these on an existing unit, stop using it and call.

  • Soot or staining near the unit.This points to poor combustion or leaking exhaust.
  • Sagging or loose pipe.Joints can open when pipe is not supported.
  • Water dripping from the vent.May point to condensate in the wrong spot.
  • Carbon monoxide alarm sounding.Go outside first, then call the gas utility or 911.
  • Error codes tied to exhaust.The unit has shut down for a reason, so do not reset repeatedly.

What this means for choosing between models

Before picking a unit, ask where it will vent and how long the run will be. That decision often matters more than the model name. Learn more on the tankless water heater page and see how plumbing installation work is planned before the first hole gets cut.
