Most hot water failures trace back to a short list: a burned-out element, a worn thermostat, a bad thermocouple or gas valve, a leaking relief valve, or a tank that has rusted through.

Do this first

Gas smell, pilot trouble or water on the floor

Handle the safety item before anything else. The repair comes after.

You smell gas.

Get everyone out of the building and avoid light switches and the garage door opener on the way. From outside, call the gas utility or 911. Call us once you are clear.

The pilot will not stay lit.

Do not keep relighting it. A pilot that goes out repeatedly can mean a bad thermocouple, a draft, or a blocked vent, and a blocked vent can push exhaust into the house. Leave the unit off and call.

Water is pooling at the base.

Close the cold supply valve on the pipe above the heater. Switch off the electric breaker or turn the gas control to off, but only if the floor around the panel or unit is dry.

The relief pipe is hissing or steaming.

Stay back from the discharge pipe, because the water coming out can scald. Turn the heater off and keep children and pets away until a plumber has looked at it.

What each symptom usually points to

The same complaint can come from different parts. These are the usual suspects, listed so you can describe the problem clearly on the phone.

No hot water at all.

On electric units, a tripped breaker, a dead upper element, or a tripped high-limit button is common. On gas units, think pilot, thermocouple or gas control valve.

Warm, never hot.

A burned-out lower element, a thermostat set too low, or a dip tube that has broken down and lets cold water mix near the top of the tank.

Rumbling or popping.

Sediment on the tank floor traps steam bubbles beneath it. A flush often quiets the noise, and heavy buildup is a sign the tank is aging.

Rust-colored hot water.

If only the hot side runs brown, suspect the tank or its anode rod. Brown on both sides points toward the supply pipes instead.

Dripping relief valve.

The valve may be worn, or pressure in the plumbing may be climbing too high. Replacing the valve without finding the cause just brings the drip back.

Hot water runs out fast.

Sediment takes up room in the tank, an element may be dead, or the household has outgrown the tank size.

Where a tank heater leaks, and which leaks are fixable

Work out where the water actually comes from, because a wet floor near a heater is not always the heater. Condensation on cold pipes, a weeping shutoff valve, or a washer hose nearby can all pool around the base.

Leaks at the top are usually repairable. Loose or corroded fittings on the inlet and outlet lines, a failed relief valve, and a dripping drain valve are parts a plumber can swap while the tank stays in service.

A leak from the bottom or from the seams of the tank body is different. Once the steel liner has rusted through, no patch holds, and the unit has to come out. If that is what we find, we say so and explain replacement options instead of selling a repair that will fail.

If the heater itself is dry and the floor is still wet, the water may be coming from a hidden pipe. That is a job for leak detection, not a heater repair. If a pipe is spraying instead, that is burst pipe repair.

Flushing and yearly checks that head off repairs

Sediment is the quiet cause of many repair calls. It sits between the flame or element and the water, so the heater works harder and its parts wear sooner.

Water here is on the soft side. The Central Weld County Water District 2025 report lists average total hardness at 33 mg/L, and that district serves much of the Tri-Town area. Soft water slows scale, so a periodic flush is usually enough, but the St. Vrain Water Authority publishes no hardness figure, so ask which supplier serves your address before you pick a schedule.

Add an anode rod check to the same visit. The rod corrodes so the tank does not, and once it is used up the steel starts to rust from the inside.
