Do this first

A failed tank is flooding the floor

Water damage grows with every minute the supply stays open. These steps limit it while a plumber is on the way.

Close the water supply.

Shut the cold inlet valve on the pipe above the heater. If it will not turn, close the main shutoff for the house instead.

Cut power or fuel, safely.

Switch off the breaker for an electric unit only if you can reach it over dry floor. If you smell gas, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 before calling us.

Keep clear of wet electrical areas.

Water near outlets, extension cords or the panel is a shock risk. Stay out and wait for the power to be shut off at the main.

Protect what you can.

Lift boxes and stored items off the floor and photograph the damage for your records. Leave heavy mopping for after the heater is dealt with.

Repair or replace: how to tell

Start with the age on the data plate, then look at where the water is coming from.

Repair usually makes sense when

  • The tank is dry and the leak is at a fitting or valve.
  • A single part such as an element or thermostat has failed.
  • The unit is fairly young and has been serviced.
  • There is no rust at the bottom seam or around the base.

Replacement usually makes sense when

  • Water is seeping through the tank body or bottom seam.
  • The unit is well past the age most tanks reach.
  • Rust-colored hot water is joined by rumbling and leaks.
  • The repair cost is close to the price of a new unit.

Why tanks fail from the bottom up

A steel tank lives under constant stress. It heats and cools many times a day, and the glass lining inside slowly cracks, which lets water reach the steel.

The anode rod is meant to take that corrosion instead. Once the rod is consumed, the tank wall starts to rust, and the first sign is often a slow weep at the base that grows over days. A new rod added early can extend a tank's life, but it cannot restore one that has already rusted through.

Sediment speeds the process. It forms hot spots on the tank floor and the lining there fails first, which is why bottom seams are where most tank leaks start. If the leak proves to be at a fitting instead, a repair visit may be all you need.

After the tank lets go: the rest of the house

A tank holds dozens of gallons, and a failure can spread water through a garage, closet or finished basement quickly. Our job is the plumbing side. Drying out walls and flooring is a separate trade, so call a water-damage company soon if drywall or carpet got wet.

Photograph the damage and the heater's data plate before anything is hauled away, and tell your insurer early. If a supply line also gave way, the scope changes, and burst pipe repair may be part of the same visit.

Decisions to make before the new unit is chosen

Most of these can be settled on the phone in a few minutes.

  • Same fuel or a change.Swapping gas for electric, or the reverse, changes the venting, the gas line and the electrical work.
  • Tank size.Match it to how many people shower and how often the dishwasher and laundry run together.
  • Where it sits.Garages, closets and attics each have clearance, drain pan and venting needs.
  • Heat pump option.Hybrid heat pump units are an option, with their own space, drain and electrical needs. We can discuss whether yours suits the location.
  • Expansion tank and valves.A closed plumbing system may need an expansion tank, and old shutoff valves are worth replacing while the lines are open.

What the replacement itself covers

Replacing like for like is the simpler path, and a planned move or fuel change has its own page on water heater installation. Replacement work is done to the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC) as adopted in Colorado, and the Firestone building division can tell you whether your swap needs a permit and inspection. Either way, the old tank is drained, disconnected, and removed as a task we agree on up front.

Be cautious about add-ons. Supply from the Central Weld County Water District is rated soft at 33 mg/L in its 2025 report, so many Firestone homes may not need a softener to protect a new tank. Test your water and ask which supplier serves you first, and read softener and filtration before buying equipment.
