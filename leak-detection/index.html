How a leak gets found without tearing up the house

Water follows pipes, framing, and slab edges before it shows up as a stain, so the wet spot you see can sit several feet from the real source. A leak finder's job is to shrink that guess down to one patch of drywall or one short trench.

The visit starts with questions. When did the bill change, which fixtures run, is there a hiss at night, which part of the floor feels warm. Then the gear comes out, matched to the pipe material and the way the lines run. Copper, plastic, and cast iron carry sound and heat differently, so the method changes with the pipe.

We also sort supply leaks from drain leaks. A supply leak keeps pressure and often hisses. A drain leak only shows when water is sent down the line, so the test is to run fixtures and watch where it appears.

Two methods usually agree before anyone cuts anything. When they do not, we run another test instead of opening a wall on a maybe.

Read your water meter to prove the leak

A high bill does not always mean a leak. Hot weather, a new sprinkler schedule, and houseguests all raise use. A bill that jumps while nothing changed is worth a meter test, and the test costs nothing but a little patience. Whether you live in ZIP code 80504 or 80520, test the meter that matches the supplier on your bill.

  1. Stop all water use.Close every tap, pause the dishwasher and washer, and turn off the sprinkler clock. Check that the ice maker is not refilling.
  2. Find and open the meter box.It usually sits in a lidded box near the street or the front of the lot. Lift the lid carefully and wipe the glass clean.
  3. Watch the small leak dial.Many meters have a triangle or star that spins when any water passes. If it moves with everything off, water is escaping somewhere past the meter.
  4. Write the numbers down.Note the full reading, wait an hour without using water, then read again. A changed number confirms a leak, and a big change hints at a big one.
  5. Close the house main and look again.If the dial keeps turning with the main shut, the leak is outside, between the meter and the house. If it stops, the leak is inside the walls, floors, or fixtures.

Stains on the ceiling and damp walls

A brown ring on a ceiling, bubbling paint, or a soft patch of drywall means water has been present for a while. Shut off the supply to the fixture above if you can and set a bucket under the stain.

If a ceiling is bulging, a small hole at its lowest point lets the water drain in a controlled way. Stand clear of the spot and any light fixture while you do it.

Bathroom overhead.

Shower pans, tub overflows, and toilet supply lines often sit right above the room that shows the stain.

Warm patch on a wall.

A warm spot on drywall can mark a hot water line, and thermal imaging shows it without a single cut.

Stain that grows after rain.

That points to roof or window water, not plumbing. We will tell you so instead of opening walls.

Stain only when a fixture runs.

That suggests a drain joint, a loose trap, or a failing seal under a tub or shower.

Musty smell with no stain.

Moisture can sit inside a wall cavity for weeks. Pressure testing or imaging finds the line before mold does.

Under the house: crawlspaces and basements

If your home has a crawlspace or basement, the pipes are exposed and the search can start there. We look for drip stains on joists, rust-streaked fittings, wet insulation, and white mineral crust where water has dried over and over. Hanging supply lines and drain joints are checked one section at a time.

Water on a basement floor is not always plumbing. A damp edge along the wall after sprinklers or rain is a drainage question, covered on our page about seepage through foundation walls. We run fixtures and read the meter first, so you know which problem you have before anyone quotes a fix.

A basement that floods from the floor drain or the sump pit is a separate case. A pump that stalls or a check valve that fails is handled under sump pump repair.

The buried line between the meter and your house

The pipe running underground from the meter to the house hides the leaks that raise a bill without leaving a stain. Clues include a meter that keeps moving with the house main closed, a strip of lawn that stays green or soggy, soft ground near the entry, and pressure that drops for no reason.

We use ground microphones to listen along the route, tracer gas to confirm small leaks, and pressure tests to prove the line is the problem. Then the dig is short and aimed. Firestone buys its treated water from Central Weld County Water District and St. Vrain Water Authority, so ask the supplier on your bill where its pipe ends and yours begins before anyone digs.

When the pipe is worn out and not just cracked in one place, a new run costs less than repeated digging. That conversation belongs to water service line replacement, and we will lay out both options. Any new run is installed to the 2021 International Plumbing Code (IPC) as adopted in Colorado, and the Firestone building division can say whether it needs a permit.

Homes on a slab: signs the leak is beneath the floor

Slab-on-grade homes carry supply lines under or inside the concrete. Slab leaks are real, but many of the clues below also have harmless causes, so a test comes before any cutting.

  • A warm spot on the floor.A hot water line leaking under tile or wood can warm one patch of floor.
  • Water sounds with everything off.A faint hiss or trickle at night, with no tap open, suggests pressure escaping somewhere.
  • Damp baseboards or lifting flooring.Moisture wicks upward, so look at the lowest trim and any flooring seams.
  • A meter that moves with taps shut.This proves water is leaving the system. The location is the next question.
  • Low hot water pressure.A pinhole on a hot line often shows up as weaker hot flow before anything gets wet.

Sprinklers, pools, and the meter box

Sprinkler lines are the most common outdoor leak. A cracked head, a failed valve, or a split buried line leaks only while a zone runs, so a soaked patch that appears on schedule points to irrigation. Run each zone and watch the meter. Some water providers send notices about annual backflow assembly testing, so ask about that device while we are on site. New devices fall under plumbing installation.

Pools and hot tubs lose water to evaporation, so a bucket test shows whether the loss is a leak. We handle the plumbing lines, valves, and equipment connections, and not the shell or liner.

Water pooling in the meter box can mean a leak on the utility side or at your shutoff valve. Do not dig around it. Call your water provider if the leak is at the meter, and call us if it is at your valve or on your side. A rural parcel around Mead may run on a well, so there may be no utility meter to read.
